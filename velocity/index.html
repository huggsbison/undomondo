Velocity Is Eating Itself

Mersenne / Undomondo · 2026 · about 10 min

"…my concern about the disappearance of an interest in time."

Harold Innis, The Bias of Communication

The late tech writer Om Malik published a piece in January called "Velocity Is the New Authority."

His diagnosis was that information used to be organised by authority. You earned attention by being right or by being first in discovery. That world is gone now and the organising principle has been replaced by velocity, the speed at which something moves through the network.

Malik was right. Once the system is tuned for speed, "authority" becomes ornamental. The network rewards whatever gets clicked, shared, quoted and remixed. Judgment comes later, if ever. But it was a short piece, written for the feed it described, and it never asked where this ends.

I.

Harold Innis was a Canadian political economist. His colleague at Toronto, the famous Marshall McLuhan, called his own The Gutenberg Galaxy a footnote to Innis. In the last years of his life Innis turned to studying how empires rise and fall through their communication media. In Empire and Communications and The Bias of Communication he argues that all media carry a bias toward either time or space.

Stone tablets, parchment and hand-copied manuscripts are time-biased. They are durable, hard to transport and built for continuity across generations. Papyrus, paper and electronic signals on the other hand are space-biased. They're light and fast, built for reach and administration.

Empires need both to survive. In Innis's words, "a stable society is dependent on an appreciation of a proper balance between the concepts of space and time." Time-bias gives you cohesion and memory. Space-bias gives you reach and control. When the balance tips too far one way, the empire cracks because its own infrastructure stops being able to sustain the civilisation it was built to serve. The correction, a rebalancing, comes from the margins: either invaders at the frontier, or a new medium the centre can't control.

A monopoly of knowledge forms around the dominant medium. Priests control who can read; scribes control what gets written. Over time the monopoly goes rigid, until a new medium arrives that it can't absorb and the whole structure fractures.

What Innis saw in 1951 is that Western civilisation was already deeply space-biased, pushed there by newspapers and radio, both optimised for reach and speed and slowly eroding the mechanisms by which societies hold themselves together over time. He called this "present-mindedness" and considered it an existential threat. His key essay was titled "A Plea for Time."

Internet, social media, algorithmic feeds, and now AI-generated content at zero marginal cost have pushed what Innis was warning about to such extreme levels that calling it "bias" understates the situation. Almost nothing in our information infrastructure favours duration or depth. The investigation that required patience doesn't travel, so the system has no use for it.

II.

McLuhan gets the credit Innis deserves, partly because he was better at writing slogans like "The medium is the message." His deeper framework, the tetrad, is more useful here. Every new medium, McLuhan argued, does four things at once: it enhances something, it obsolesces something, it retrieves something from the past, and when pushed to its limit, it reverses into something else.

Malik's essay was, with or without knowing it, about the first two quadrants. Velocity enhances reach and obsolesces authority.

So what does velocity-as-organising-principle retrieve? Oral culture.

The meme is how a story travels now. Compressed and contextless, passed along as gist. You don't need to read the whole thing. You just need the version of the thing that fits in a sentence as an image or a joke. This is similar to how information moved in pre-literate societies, by rumour and reputation, with stories mutating with each retelling. McLuhan called this the "global village," and he didn't mean it warmly. He meant tribalism at scale, the paranoia of small-town gossip networks operating at planetary reach.

Velocity only brought back the form of oral culture and not the function. Before writing, rhythm and metre did the work of memory. The song was how you stored your tribe's code and history, your dead and your gods. You remembered the song because the song was the instrument to remember everything else.

Recording and then streaming made music portable, less and less tied to a specific body in a specific room. Terminal velocity arrives now with Suno and AI generation. By Suno's own numbers, it makes seven million songs a day, the size of Spotify's entire catalogue every two weeks. Sheldon Pearce recently caught the downstream effect in The New Yorker: albums now arrive as "an anticlimactic drop in an ocean overflowing with too much content." You don't buy records anymore. You subscribe to everything ever made. The album becomes "a menu instead of a meal."

The oral tradition Innis admired, where rhythm carried cultural identity across centuries, has been inverted into a content firehose.

III.

That leaves us with the fourth operation. What does velocity reverse into when pushed to its extreme?

Neil Postman had part of the answer in Amusing Ourselves to Death, his 1985 book about television. The public discourse was turning into entertainment and nobody was noticing it because it was pleasant. His bet was that Huxley's dystopia would arrive first: we'd be undone by what we love.

We're being busied to death by a feed that demands activity. Scroll, react and share. None of it requires an ounce of comprehension. We are in perpetual motion without a destination, and we're not even being entertained to death anymore. Postman saw this coming in 1992: information "directed at no one in particular, in enormous volume and at high speeds, and disconnected from theory, meaning, or purpose." That's a description of the feed before it existed.

Push it all to its conclusion and you arrive at Baudrillard's Fatal Strategies. When a system gets fast and saturated enough, it implodes into meaninglessness. Elsewhere he called it the "ecstasy of communication." His analogy for this process was pornography, which kills desire through total exposure. His answer was to push the system's logic to its extreme until it collapses or reverses.

The platform monopoly of knowledge right now is behavioural prediction. Instagram, TikTok, and Spotify all depend on it. What they sell now is the ability to predict what you'll do next and monetise the prediction. The same logic runs openly in Polymarket and Kalshi. Prediction is present-mindedness turned into a business where the only future it cares about is your next ten seconds.

This is the space-biased monopoly of our era. Every platform is racing to perfect it with zero-cost generation and infinite velocity through AI. It's the fatal strategy running on its own.

IV.

There are elegant counter-arguments to this condition. One, written by Kevin Kelly twenty years ago and reposted this February, says the feeling of technological singularity, that acceleration is approaching infinity and something unprecedented is about to happen, is a mathematical phantom. He says the singularity is always near, and always will be. It's a permanent structural illusion baked into exponential growth. Plot any exponential curve on an ordinary chart and the end always looks vertical. His conclusion is to relax, because this feeling has always been normal.

Innis, however, would read the same data differently. The feeling of approaching singularity is a symptom of extreme space-bias. It's what it feels like to live in a civilisation so dominated by speed and reach that time-depth has been crushed out of experience. Of course it feels like acceleration toward infinity; our media infrastructure has eliminated every mechanism for perceiving time any other way than "faster." We can't tell anymore if we're really speeding up.

Previous moments of apparent singularity still had time-bias retained somewhere in the system. The university, the guild and the monastery were durable institutions that counterweighed the space-bias of whatever new medium was emerging. Monasteries were hierarchical and exclusionary, with their own monopolies of knowledge. But they were durable, and durability decides what knowledge survives a collapse. Postman, in Technopoly, called such institutions a culture's immune system. Their job is to keep "a balance between the old and the new," and they do it by "destroying" unwanted information. Take them away and you get what he called "information glut."

In March a public TV station in St. Louis lost access to seventy years of archive when its cloud vendor disappeared. We're now tearing the counterweights out.

As AI pushes velocity toward its logical endpoint with infinite speed and zero cost, people have started distrusting everything, and not just the AI kind. This year the Reuters Institute found trust in news at 37%, the lowest it has recorded, while 62% of people worry about telling what is real from what is fake online. The advice is to "assume that everything you see online is fake until proven otherwise." Spike got there from the image side this spring: "All Images Are Quite Useless Now."

When generation costs drop to zero, speed stops being a differentiator. What becomes scarce then? Time spent with the thing. The review written twenty years after the record came out by someone who never stopped playing it. Perhaps the rise of the listening bar is a symptom of that scarcity. The Japanese kissaten's room and horn speakers have been successfully copied from London to Barcelona, yet in most of the copies people talk over the record. Innis wrote that "a concern with communication by the ear assumes reliance on time." The thing the algorithm buried for speed is exactly what it makes valuable.

V.

Rebalancing starts small, and it rarely starts by beating the dominant medium on its own terms. Any strategy that depends on awakening people to their capture is fighting the wrong battle. People feed the machine because they want to be seen, a need older than any medium; the medium only decides how long being seen lasts. You can't argue someone out of the medium they live inside because transitions are invisible from within. But you can build the place from which the shift becomes visible, in retrospect.

Pushing things further, as Baudrillard advised, won't advance us either. The platforms are doing that work on their own and they don't need our help. Alexander Galloway and Eugene Thacker give another piece of advice in The Exploit (2007), which is to find the holes in a network's protocols and push change through them. The swarm they describe breaks things, and breaking things is half the job. Without a slow institution to keep things alive it's vandalism, and it just clears ground for the next space-biased monopoly to grow in. A rebalancing needs both. Their book says the new exploit will be an "antiweb." It "has yet to be invented, but the newly ascendant network sovereigns will likely breed the antiweb into existence, just as the old twentieth-century powers bred their own demise." I've been trying to understand what antiweb means since I first read it, and by now the only thing I could come up with is the ordinary routines that go around the algorithm.

VI.

So what's a time-biased medium now?

Almost nothing that still counts as media in the formal sense. What's left of time-bias survives as practices embedded in physical life.

Knowledge passing through voice and relationship in a room, inseparable from its context. A piano teacher showing a student where to place their fingers. This is the guild's old shape, the master and the apprentice, the knowledge earned slowly. You could record every lesson and put it on YouTube and it would transmit almost none of what actually transfers between two people in that room. Because the knowledge lives in the relationship and accrues over years, and it can't be compressed or distributed.

Fermentation is time made edible. It can't be accelerated past a certain point. The knowledge of how to do it well is embodied and experiential, learned by ruining batches over months and years.

Then the harder version: gardens and farms, the long establishment of soil. Jem Bendell's Breaking Together documents people who responded to the awareness of civilisational collapse by starting farms, joining ecovillages, building local exchange systems as practical adaptations to an already failing system. When tourism collapsed in Bali during the pandemic, many people went back to their home villages and family plots and grew some of their own food. Most people in the "advanced" economies have no such fallback. That's the difference between a civilisation with some time-bias left in its infrastructure and one optimised fully for space.

None of these compete with space-biased media on reach or speed. The velocity system can only capture their image. You can post the kimchi, but the part that matters stays in the jar. Their value is inseparable from duration.

What we need now is a modern analogue of the monastery and the university, and it will start small, around ordinary things like these. The originals did get big in the end, but only after they had lasted for centuries.

So, back to Malik. I would have liked to read his reply. He called this the new authority. Innis would have called it the terminal phase of a monopoly. Distrust is all I can show so far. My bet is that the medium has fulfilled its bias so completely that it's starting to reverse.

Slow things don't need to scale. They need to last.

What are we building now?

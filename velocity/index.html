Velocity Is Eating Itself

Mersenne / Undomondo · 2026 · about 12 min

"…my concern about the disappearance of an interest in time."

Harold Innis, The Bias of Communication

"In a system tuned for speed, authority is ornamental," the late tech writer Om Malik wrote in January, in "Velocity Is the New Authority."

His diagnosis was that information used to be organised by authority. You earned attention by being right or by being first in discovery. That world is gone now and the organising principle has been replaced by velocity, the speed at which something moves through the network. The network rewards whatever gets clicked, shared, quoted and remixed, and judgment comes later, "if ever."

I read it as someone who has been terminally online and running a music blog for two decades, and it described what I'd watched happen to online discourse. For me, it left one question open, though: where does a system tuned for speed end up?

I went back to three media theorists for the answer: Harold Innis, Marshall McLuhan and Neil Postman. My answer is distrust, and whatever grows after it will be slow.

I.

Harold Innis, the Canadian political economist at the University of Toronto, turned to studying how empires rise and fall through their communication media in his last years. In Empire and Communications and The Bias of Communication, he argues that all media carry a bias toward either time or space.

Time-biased technologies like stone tablets, parchment and manuscripts are durable and built for continuity across generations, yet hard to transport. Papyrus, paper and electronic signals on the other hand are space-biased. They're light and fast, built for reach and administration.

Empires need both to survive. In Innis's words, "a stable society is dependent on an appreciation of a proper balance between the concepts of space and time." Time-bias gives you cohesion and memory. Space-bias gives you reach and control. When the balance tips too far one way, the empire cracks because its own infrastructure can no longer sustain the civilisation it was built to serve.

What Innis saw in 1951 is that Western civilisation was already deeply space-biased, pushed there by newspapers and radio, both optimised for reach and speed and slowly eroding the mechanisms by which societies hold themselves together over time. He called this "present-mindedness." Of the press, the radio and the cinema he wrote that "time was destroyed and it became increasingly difficult to achieve continuity or to ask for a consideration of the future." His key essay was titled "A Plea for Time."

The internet, and now AI-generated content, have pushed what Innis was warning about to such extremes that calling it "bias" understates the situation. Almost nothing in our information infrastructure favours duration or depth. Anything that took patience to make doesn't travel, so the system has no use for it.

Innis had a name for whoever controls the winning medium: a monopoly of knowledge. Priests control who can read; scribes control what gets written. Over time the monopoly goes rigid, until a new medium arrives that it can't absorb and the whole structure fractures. The correction, a rebalancing, comes from the margins: either invaders at the frontier, or a new medium the centre can't control.

II.

Marshall McLuhan, seventeen years younger than Innis, joined the same university in 1946 and later called his own The Gutenberg Galaxy a footnote to Innis. Yet McLuhan is the one people remember, partly because he was better at slogans like "the medium is the message." Every new medium, McLuhan argued, does four things at once: it enhances something, it obsolesces something, it retrieves something from the past, and when pushed to its limit, it reverses into something else.

Malik, knowingly or not, wrote about the first two. Velocity enhances reach and obsolesces authority.

So what does velocity retrieve? Oral culture. Innis counted it as time-biased, because a society that remembers by voice has to keep what it knows alive in its people, its memory.

Before writing, rhythm and metre did the work. The song was how you stored your tribe's code and history, your dead and your gods. You remembered the song because it was the instrument to remember everything else. Velocity brought back the form of oral culture and broke its function. Its unit is the meme.

Compressed and contextless, it is passed along as gist. You don't need to read the whole thing. You just need the version of the thing that fits in a sentence as an image or a joke. It moves the same way stories did before writing, by rumour and reputation, mutating with each retelling. McLuhan called this the "global village," and he didn't mean it warmly. He meant tribalism at scale, the paranoia of small-town gossip networks operating at planetary reach.

The song is the oldest memory device we have, yet the feed treats it as background sound. It kept the form it had in oral culture, short and repeated and passed along, but the meme has taken over the job and the song took on the meme's habits. Sheldon Pearce recently caught the effect in The New Yorker: albums now arrive as "an anticlimactic drop in an ocean overflowing with too much content," and the album becomes "a menu instead of a meal."

AI generation is taking it further. Suno alone, according to a leaked investor deck, makes seven million songs a day, the size of Spotify's entire catalogue every two weeks. The oral tradition Innis admired, where rhythm carried cultural identity across centuries, has been inverted into a content firehose.

A song can still carry something true, but nobody can take in seven million new ones a day, so what gets through is whatever hits fastest and it's forgotten just as fast.

III.

What does velocity reverse into when pushed to its extreme? My answer is distrust, and Postman shows the road there.

Back in the age of television, Neil Postman argued that public discourse was turning into entertainment and nobody noticed because it was pleasant. His bet was that Huxley's dystopia would arrive first: we'd be entertained to death. It's worse now. We're being busied to death by a feed that demands activity. We scroll and react. None of it requires an ounce of comprehension. We are in perpetual motion without a destination.

In 1992 Postman described information "directed at no one in particular, in enormous volume and at high speeds, and disconnected from theory, meaning, or purpose." That's a description of the feed before it existed.

The feed looks like the opposite of the priests' monopoly. In the early days of the internet it gave a false sense of liberation, because you could scroll in any direction. But today it's the feed that decides your next ten seconds through what the platforms hold: knowledge of you. TikTok and Spotify run on predicting what you will do next, and they make their money from being right. A business built on predicting your next click has no use for a future further off than that. We see the same pattern with the prediction markets. Since February you can bet on Polymarket on whether Bitcoin closes up or down in the next five minutes, so the future they price is the same length as the feed.

A system tuned for speed has nothing in it to slow it down. Once generation costs nothing, everything arrives at once and nothing has time to be checked. That's where velocity reverses: into distrust.

This year the Reuters Institute found trust in news at 37%, the lowest it has recorded, while 62% say they are concerned about fake news online. The writer merritt k's advice is to assume that everything you see online is fake until proven otherwise. The art magazine Spike got there from the image side this spring: "All Images Are Quite Useless Now." Distrust is the first reversal, the collapse. The second is what grows in the rubble, a return of time-bias.

IV.

There are elegant counter-arguments to this condition. One, written by Kevin Kelly twenty years ago and reposted this February, says the feeling of technological singularity, that acceleration is approaching infinity and something unprecedented is about to happen, is a mathematical phantom. He says the singularity is always near, and always will be. It's a permanent structural illusion baked into exponential growth. Plot any exponential curve on an ordinary chart and the end always looks vertical. His conclusion is that the feeling is meaningless, and that real transitions look ordinary in hindsight.

Kelly is right about the maths, but had Innis been reading it, he might have said that the feeling of approaching singularity is a symptom of extreme space-bias. It's what it feels like to live in a civilisation so dominated by speed and reach that time-depth has been crushed out of experience. Of course it feels like acceleration toward infinity; our media infrastructure has squeezed out most of the ways we had of feeling time as anything but "faster." We can't tell anymore if we're really speeding up.

Previous moments of apparent singularity still had time-bias retained somewhere in the system. The monastery and the early university were durable institutions that counterweighed the space-bias of whatever new medium was emerging. True, monasteries were hierarchical and exclusionary, with their own monopolies of knowledge, but they were durable, and durability decides what knowledge survives a collapse. Postman, in Technopoly, called such institutions a culture's immune system. Their job is to keep "a balance between the old and the new," and they do it by "destroying" unwanted information. Take them away and you get what he called "information glut."

We're now tearing the counterweights out, and fast. USAID took sixty-four years to build and under six months to shut down. It held knowledge the way a guild does: in people who spent careers working in the same countries and trained the ones who came after. Its documents can be saved. What can't be saved is what those decades of postings taught its people, like who in which ministry can get a shipment through. That lived in people and in the trust between them, and no archive or training set can give it back.

Most people already distrust what they see and stay anyway. People feed the machine because they want to be seen, a need older than any medium; the medium only decides how long being seen lasts. That's why any strategy that depends on awakening people to their capture won't work. You can't argue someone out of the medium they live inside. But you can build the place from which the shift becomes visible, in retrospect.

The coming rebalancing, the correction Innis described, comes from the margins, starts small, and it rarely starts by beating the dominant medium on its own terms. The opposite move is to speed everything up until it breaks. Baudrillard argued for it in Fatal Strategies, and Silicon Valley's accelerationists (e/acc, "effective accelerationism") argue for it now with venture money. It's a useful tactic, because it shows where the system is weak. But it can't be where we end up. A platform that makes everything for free wins any race on speed, and the platforms are already accelerating on their own.

Galloway and Thacker's The Exploit makes the same bet from inside the network. A network runs on rules, and rules have weak points, so you find them the way a hacker does and push change through them. That breaks things, but breaking things is half the job. Without a slow institution like a library or a record shop that has been open for thirty years, to keep things alive, it's vandalism, and it just clears ground for the next space-biased monopoly.

The better half of the same text is one line. The resistance will need a new form, which Galloway and Thacker called the antiweb, and nobody has invented it yet. I've been haunted by that line ever since, but I haven't been able to picture the fourth dimension from a three-dimensional world. Every escape so far has been bought back: by the time the young had moved from Facebook to Instagram, Facebook owned Instagram. The more I think about it, the only answer I can come up with is slow things, the ordinary routines that go around the algorithm. The routines last because they're too small to be worth buying.

V.

So what's a time-biased medium now?

Almost nothing that still counts as media in the formal sense. What's left of time-bias survives as practices embedded in physical life.

When generation costs drop to zero, speed stops being a differentiator. What becomes scarce then? Time spent with the thing. The review written twenty years after the record came out by someone who never stopped playing it.

Knowledge passing through voice and relationship in a room, inseparable from its context. A piano teacher showing a student where to place their fingers. You could record every lesson and put it on YouTube and it would carry almost none of what passes between two people in that room, because the knowledge lives in the relationship. This is the guild's old shape, the master and the apprentice. Michael Polanyi wrote in 1958 that "to learn by example is to submit to authority." The student trusts the teacher long before they can judge them. That's authority too, the kind Malik didn't mention, and it's built over years in one room.

Think of fermentation. It can't be accelerated past a certain point. The knowledge of how to do it well is embodied and experiential, learned by ruining batches over months and years.

Gardens and farms are slower still, because soil takes years to establish. Jem Bendell's Breaking Together documents people who answered the awareness of civilisational collapse by starting farms and joining ecovillages. When tourism collapsed in Bali during the pandemic, many people went back to their home villages and family plots and grew some of their own food. Most people in the "advanced" economies have no such fallback, and couldn't tell you which country the carrots in their fridge came from. That's the difference between a civilisation with some time-bias left in its infrastructure and one optimised fully for space.

None of these compete with space-biased media on reach or speed. The feed can only capture their image. You can post the kimchi on Instagram, but the part that matters stays in the jar.

The rise of the listening bar is a symptom of the same scarcity. The Japanese originals have been copied from London to Barcelona, the hi-fi systems and vinyl selectors, and a slow hour with a record. However in most copies I've been to people keep talking over the record. The format and the room can be copied in a month. Yet the habit of listening has to be built by the people in it, and that takes years. Innis wrote that "a concern with communication by the ear assumes reliance on time."

That doesn't have to mean the only way out is offline. A blog kept for twenty years, or a forum where the same people are still arguing in the same thread, builds up because people keep coming back to it. Although servers are rented, and a St. Louis TV station lost seventy years of archive this March when its cloud vendor cut it off, this year I brought Undomondo's archive back: four thousand posts from the WordPress and Tumblr years, which had sat in a zip file since 2015, because a decade ago I had put my trust in the feed.

I'm not the only one. I've noticed the beloved Lovefingers blog has brought its archive back, and so has another blog which I chanced upon on Reddit. Nothing in the feed rewards any of this, so why are several of us bringing the archives back at the same time, without having agreed on it? I think this is what the start of a rebalancing looks like: a few people defending small corners of the internet against link rot.

What we need now is something that does what the monastery and the early university once did, and it will start small, around ordinary things like these. The originals did get big in the end, but only after they had lasted for centuries.

So, back to Malik. I would have liked to read his reply. He called velocity the new authority. Innis would have called it the terminal phase of a monopoly of knowledge, the point where a medium has fulfilled its bias so completely that it starts to reverse. The distrust is already here and a few people are rebuilding their archives.

Slow things don't need to scale. They need to last.

What are we building now?
